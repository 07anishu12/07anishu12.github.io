Used EV Battery Health: What to Check Before Buying

Check SOH evidence, charging behaviour, warranty and inspection limits before buying a used EV.

Check a used electric car's battery through a manufacturer-supported diagnostic assessment, documented warranty terms and a controlled inspection of charging and operation. Dashboard range alone is not a battery-health certificate. State of charge tells you how full the battery is now; state of health concerns its condition relative to an appropriate new-battery reference.

Identify the exact battery and evidence

Record model, manufacturing year, battery variant, kilometres and any replacement history. Similar model names can cover different packs. Tata.ev support material, for example, distinguishes several model and battery families, so an older article's specification may not match the used car.

Ask an authorised workshop whether it can provide a written assessment and what that assessment measures. A report should identify the vehicle, date, method and relevant limitations. An unexplained percentage printed by an unknown app is weaker evidence than a traceable manufacturer-supported diagnostic process.

Do not assume every brand offers the same consumer-facing SOH report. Confirm availability before paying a non-refundable booking amount. Direct retrieval of the Tata support host had access limitations during research, so verify current model-specific documents independently.

Understand SOH without overinterpreting one number

SOH can refer to capacity-related or other condition estimates under a particular method. Ask what the percentage is compared against: nominal energy, original usable energy or another reference. Figures from different tools may not be directly comparable.

For a hypothetical pack that originally provided 40 kWh of usable energy, a properly measured comparable result of 36 kWh implies 90% capacity retention. That arithmetic does not establish the battery's power capability, cell balance, safety or remaining life.

A dashboard showing 250 km after a full charge is not the same calculation. Estimated range also reflects recent driving, temperature, climate control and software assumptions. A software update or a change in trip pattern can alter the estimate without an equivalent change in physical battery capacity.

Inspect charging and diagnostic history

Ask about previous warning messages, repairs, charging interruptions and battery replacements, then compare the answers with invoices and workshop records. Frequent public fast charging is a question to explore, not proof by itself that the pack is damaged.

Arrange a qualified inspection of the underbody and battery enclosure for damage, corrosion or evidence of repairs. Do not open a high-voltage pack or probe its terminals yourself. A normal-looking exterior cannot rule out internal faults, while a diagnostic scan also has limits.

With permission and suitable equipment, check the charging types the car supports and watch for errors or unexpected interruption. A brief successful plug-in does not establish the full charging curve or remaining capacity. Record conditions rather than turning one session into an unqualified health verdict.

Run a sensible range check

If the workshop considers a road assessment appropriate, use a known route, recorded starting and ending charge, consistent driving and a safe reserve. Note temperature, speed, load and climate-control use. Do not deliberately run the battery empty to prove a seller's range claim.

Suppose, hypothetically, a journey uses 12 kWh over 80 km, giving 0.15 kWh/km. A separately established 30 kWh usable-energy estimate would imply 200 km before reserve under those same conditions. Neither input is a measurement of the car you are considering.

This illustrates why consumption and capacity must be considered separately. A short range result can reflect high consumption rather than degradation; an apparently impressive gentle urban result does not establish highway capability. Compare like conditions and retain uncertainty.

Read warranty transfer and price the remaining risk

Obtain written battery-warranty terms applicable to that VIN and ownership history. Tata's warranty material uses SOH-related conditions, illustrating that a warranty has defined thresholds and remedies rather than a vague promise of an always-new battery.

Do not assume first-owner, lifetime or promotional coverage transfers unchanged. Check years, kilometres, exclusions, required servicing, ownership conditions and whether repair or replacement is the contractual remedy. A replacement pack may also have specific warranty treatment.

Ask for a current model-specific repair assessment if a fault is found, not an unsupported internet replacement-cost estimate. Compare the asking price with better-documented vehicles and keep a contingency. Walk away if ownership, serious damage or unresolved high-voltage faults cannot be satisfactorily assessed.

Quick questions, clear answers

Is 100% charge the same as 100% battery health?

No. State of charge describes present fullness under the vehicle's control system. A degraded battery can still display fully charged. Health assessment requires a suitable reference, method and diagnostic context; the two percentages should not be treated as interchangeable.

Does a high SOH guarantee no future failure?

No. A capacity estimate does not establish every aspect of cell condition, electronics or future reliability. Consider faults, damage, repair history and warranty alongside it. Ask the assessor to explain the report's limits rather than treating one number as a guarantee.

Should I rely on the remaining warranty alone?

No. Verify that coverage applies to you and understand exclusions and remedies. Warranty support may reduce some financial risk, but downtime, eligibility disputes and uncovered damage remain possible. A documented inspection and suitable purchase price still matter.
