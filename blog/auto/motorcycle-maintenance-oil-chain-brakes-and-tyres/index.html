A useful motorcycle maintenance schedule begins with the exact owner's manual, then adds regular condition checks and work appropriate to your use. There is no single safe oil-change, chain-slack or tyre-pressure setting for all bikes. Record both time and kilometres, and have safety-critical faults assessed before riding again.

Start with the exact model and service table

Identify model, production year, engine and variant. Use official sources such as Honda's owner-manual portal to locate the right document. A manual for a related engine or previous generation may specify different parts, intervals or procedures.

Copy the applicable time-and-distance schedule into a calendar or spreadsheet. Where the manufacturer says whichever comes first, a lightly used bike can still need time-based work. Check the first-service requirements separately rather than assuming the normal repeating interval applies immediately.

Note any severe-use provisions for dust, water exposure, traffic or other conditions described by the manufacturer. Do not invent a universal severe-service percentage reduction. Ask authorised support how the official schedule applies to your actual use if the wording is unclear.

Check oil and fluids without guessing specifications

Use the prescribed oil viscosity, performance standard and quantity for the actual engine. Wet-clutch compatibility can matter, so a car oil or an unapproved thinner grade should not be chosen solely because it is cheaper or advertised as efficient.

Follow the manual's method for checking level, including posture, temperature and settling time. A sight glass read on the wrong slope or a dipstick checked using the wrong procedure can mislead. Too much oil is not a substitute for correct maintenance.

Look for leaks and unusual consumption, and check coolant or other fluids where fitted under the stated procedure. Do not open a hot cooling system or ignore warning lights while waiting for the next scheduled service. Retain invoices identifying the actual oil and parts used.

Maintain the drive system appropriately

For a chain-drive bike, inspect condition, lubrication and slack using the manufacturer's specified position and method. Do not apply a slack figure from another motorcycle. Check different chain positions as instructed because wear can be uneven.

Clean and lubricate using compatible products and procedures. Keep fingers, clothing and tools away from a moving drivetrain; do not improvise powered-wheel cleaning. Excess tension can create problems just as excessive slack can, so seek proper adjustment if unsure.

Inspect sprockets, alignment and associated fasteners under the service guidance. Belt- or shaft-drive motorcycles have different requirements; a universal chain-care checklist should not be applied to them. Replace worn components according to qualified assessment rather than appearance alone.

Make tyres and brakes a routine priority

Michelin's motorcycle guidance directs owners to vehicle-manufacturer pressure recommendations and professional assessment of punctures. Check cold pressures for the actual load and approved fitment, plus tread, damage and recurring pressure loss.

Do not bleed air from hot tyres simply to force them down to a cold specification. The tyre's moulded maximum-pressure marking is not the normal road-use setting for your motorcycle. A wider replacement tyre also needs manufacturer approval rather than an assumption that more width means more safety.

Check brake feel, fluid where relevant, pads or shoes, leaks and unusual sounds under the manual. A warning light or sudden loss of braking performance needs prompt assessment. Avoid riding to prove a symptom or spraying lubricants near braking surfaces.

Keep records and distinguish checks from repairs

Create columns for date, odometer, task, findings, parts, workshop and next due point. Mark inspection separately from replacement. A mechanic checking an air filter does not mean it was replaced, and a free-service visit may still have chargeable parts or fluids.

For a hypothetical bike whose manual specifies a task every 6,000 km or six months, a rider covering 2,000 km in six months would follow the time limit. This is an illustration of whichever-comes-first logic, not a service interval recommendation for your motorcycle.

Before a long trip, review the schedule and condition early enough to resolve findings. Check lights, horn, controls and luggage securement as well as consumables. A last-minute oil change alone does not establish touring readiness.

Learn the simple checks your manual authorises, but recognise the limits of your tools and skill. Steering, brakes, wheels, suspension and fuel-system work can require correct procedures and torque settings.

Quick questions, clear answers

Should low-mileage bikes still be serviced?

Follow the manual's time limits as well as distance. Storage, ageing fluids and other conditions can matter even when kilometres are low. Do not assume that a motorcycle parked for months needs no checks before being returned to use.

Can I use a universal chain-slack setting?

No. Suspension geometry and checking procedures vary. Use the exact manual's specification and measurement method, and have uneven wear or adjustment uncertainty assessed. A figure copied from another bike can leave the chain dangerously tight or excessively loose.

Does a free service include every consumable?

Read the actual terms. Labour, inspections, oil, filters and wear parts may be treated differently. Ask for an estimate and an itemised invoice, then record what was checked and replaced rather than assuming the service label describes all work.
