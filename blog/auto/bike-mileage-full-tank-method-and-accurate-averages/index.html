Bike mileage in km/l is distance travelled divided by fuel consumed over a comparable full-tank interval. Fill consistently under the manufacturer's safety instructions, record the odometer and litres, and combine several intervals. A fuel-gauge bar or a short dashboard reading is not an accurate substitute for measured fuel quantity.

Set up a safe, repeatable measurement

Read the correct owner's manual for filling posture and safe fill level. Honda's official manual portal illustrates where model-specific instructions belong. Do not repeatedly top off to the brim, spill fuel or use an unsafe balancing arrangement to make the reading look precise.

Use a dependable pump and a consistent method where practical, recognising that exact repeatability is limited. Record date, odometer reading, litres added and amount paid. Reset the trip meter if convenient, but keep the odometer reading as a cross-check.

The initial fill establishes the starting condition; it does not by itself measure the fuel used in the next interval. Ride normally rather than making an artificial economy run unless you clearly label it as a separate experiment.

Calculate the next refill correctly

At the following comparable full fill, record the distance since the starting fill and the litres needed to restore the same fill condition. Mileage = distance ÷ litres. The fuel added at the ending fill estimates the consumption over that interval.

Suppose a hypothetical bike travels 312 km and takes 6.5 litres to refill. Mileage is 312 ÷ 6.5 = 48 km/l. If petrol was ₹100/litre, the fuel expense for that interval is ₹650 and cost per kilometre ₹650 ÷ 312, or about ₹2.08.

These figures are illustrative, not a model's measured result or a current pump price. Litres and rupees answer different questions: mileage measures efficiency, while cost per kilometre also depends on fuel price. Keep both in the log.

Combine intervals using totals

Do not take a simple average of individual km/l readings unless the fuel quantities make that appropriate. The robust combined calculation is total distance divided by total litres over the measured intervals.

For example, a hypothetical first interval covers 100 km using 2 litres, or 50 km/l. A second covers 240 km using 6 litres, or 40 km/l. Combined mileage is 340 ÷ 8 = 42.5 km/l, not the simple average of 45 km/l.

If you buy a partial refill between two full fills, include those litres when calculating the complete interval. Ignoring an intermediate purchase makes the final full-tank result falsely impressive. If a quantity or reading is missing, mark the interval incomplete rather than inventing it.

Record conditions that explain variation

Note city/highway mix, traffic, pillion load, hills, short-trip frequency and significant weather differences. A cold-start-heavy week is not directly comparable with a steady long ride. Do not attribute every change to a new fuel, accessory or service without controlling other factors.

Keep tyres at the manufacturer-recommended cold pressure for the load. Michelin's guidance emphasises manufacturer pressure and professional assessment of tyre problems. Overinflating to chase a mileage figure can compromise the vehicle's intended behaviour.

Check that the odometer is functioning normally and that tyre sizes match approved specifications. Distance readings have limitations too. A phone's GPS can provide context, but it is not automatically exact on every route or a reason to alter vehicle settings.

Interpret changes without unsafe experiments

Look for a persistent shift across comparable intervals rather than reacting to one fill. Small differences can come from fill-level variation. No fixed percentage of improvement or deterioration can be diagnosed universally from the calculation alone.

If economy worsens alongside warning lights, rough running, leaks, brake drag or tyre problems, follow the manual and seek qualified assessment. Do not continue riding an unsafe vehicle merely to finish a data series. A fuel log supports diagnosis; it does not replace inspection.

Use the result to budget conservatively and compare your own riding patterns. A manufacturer's certification claim and your full-tank road result follow different conditions, so a lower figure is not by itself proof of a defect. Avoid public-road speed runs or extreme coasting in pursuit of a publishable number.

Quick questions, clear answers

Can I measure using reserve-to-reserve distance?

Reserve indications and pickup behaviour can vary with slope, motion and the vehicle's design. Without a reliable measured fuel quantity, the result is less controlled. Prefer comparable safe fill points and recorded litres, following your model's instructions.

Should I average the displayed mileage numbers?

For full-tank records, divide total distance by total litres. A simple average of separate km/l values can be wrong when intervals use different fuel quantities. Keep raw readings so that another person can reproduce your calculation.

Does premium fuel automatically improve mileage?

No improvement is established here. Use the fuel grade specified by the manufacturer and evaluate any change with comparable repeated records. Different traffic or fill levels can create an apparent benefit that is unrelated to the fuel itself.
