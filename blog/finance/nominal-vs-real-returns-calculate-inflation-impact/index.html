Nominal vs Real Returns: How Inflation Reduces Your Investment Gains

Calculate inflation-adjusted returns and distinguish account growth from purchasing power.

Nominal return measures growth in money; real return measures growth in purchasing power. The exact one-period relationship is Real return = (1 + nominal return)/(1 + inflation) - 1. Subtracting inflation is a useful approximation at modest rates, but the ratio is more accurate. Match the periods and include costs and taxes consistently.

Understand what inflation changes

If an account rises from ₹1,00,000 to ₹1,08,000, its nominal return is 8%. If the goods you need become 6% more expensive, the extra ₹8,000 does not translate into an 8% improvement in purchasing power. The same basket that cost ₹1,00,000 now costs ₹1,06,000.

SEBI's inflation explainer explains that rising prices reduce what a fixed sum can buy. It does not mean every household faces the same inflation rate. Rent, education, healthcare and transport can have different price changes and different weights in your budget.

Use a published inflation measure for a broad historical comparison, while modelling specific goal costs separately where appropriate. Do not call a hypothetical 6% assumption the current Indian inflation rate. A forecast assumption, a measured index reading and your household's experienced inflation are different things.

Work through the exact calculation

Using the hypothetical 8% investment return and 6% inflation, real return is 1.08/1.06-1, or approximately 1.89%. The simple subtraction gives 2%. The difference is small here, but increases when rates are higher.

In Excel place nominal return in B2 and inflation in B3, both formatted as percentages. Use =(1+B2)/(1+B3)-1. A useful cross-check is to divide the ending balance by the inflation growth factor. ₹1,08,000 divided by 1.06 equals about ₹1,01,887 in starting-period purchasing power.

For a five-year illustration, ₹2,00,000 growing at a constant 8% becomes about ₹2,93,866. With inflation at a constant 6%, divide by 1.06^5 to express the ending value in today's rupees. The result is approximately ₹2,19,593, showing real cumulative growth of about 9.80%.

These constant-rate calculations are teaching scenarios. They neither predict investment performance nor establish what inflation will be. Use a scenario table with several assumptions when the purpose is planning a future goal.

Include taxes without inventing tax treatment

Suppose a hypothetical product earns 8% and an assumed effective tax drag reduces the investor's net return to 5.6%. Against 6% inflation, real return becomes 1.056/1.06-1, approximately minus 0.38%. The account grows in rupees while purchasing power declines.

The assumed tax drag is not a statement of current Indian law. Different investments can have different taxable events, exemptions, withholding rules and timing. An annual tax haircut is not equivalent to a capital-gains tax paid only when an asset is sold. Use the actual product's applicable treatment for a real decision.

Similarly, deduct expenses only if your nominal return is before those expenses. NAV-based mutual fund returns usually already reflect scheme expenses. An accurate inflation adjustment cannot rescue an inconsistent starting return figure.

Use real returns for goals, not as a guarantee

A retirement target expressed in today's rupees can be projected using real returns, provided every cash flow is treated consistently. Alternatively inflate the future expenses and discount or invest using nominal returns. Mixing a nominal growth rate with an uninflated future spending target understates the required corpus.

For irregular investments, calculate a nominal money-weighted return from dated flows, then consider how inflation affects each contribution and withdrawal. Applying a single current inflation reading to a decade-long investment history is not a reliable historical real-return calculation.

The practical next step is to build two columns: rupee balances and purchasing-power balances. Record the inflation source, period and assumptions. If a supposedly safe investment protects nominal principal but fails to support the goal after inflation, identify that shortfall explicitly rather than labelling the product simply safe or unsafe.

Quick questions, clear answers

Can a positive return be a loss in real terms?

Yes. If the relevant price level rises faster than the after-cost return, purchasing power falls even when the account balance increases. This is particularly important for long periods, when a small annual shortfall compounds into a meaningful reduction in what savings can buy.

Should I always subtract inflation from CAGR?

Use the exact ratio with an inflation rate annualised over the same period. Subtraction is an approximation. For changing yearly rates, compound the investment and inflation factors separately. Do not mix one year's inflation with a multi-year CAGR and describe it as measured historical real performance.

Does gold or equity guarantee inflation protection?

No investment here is presented as a guaranteed inflation hedge. Prices, business outcomes and valuations can produce losses over relevant holding periods. Diversification and a suitable horizon can help manage uncertainty, but the actual goal must be tested under less favourable assumptions too.
