Build a goal-based asset allocation and understand diversification, rebalancing and risk.

Asset allocation is the division of a portfolio across asset types such as equity, debt, gold and cash. It should reflect when you need the money and the losses you can withstand. Diversification reduces dependence on one source of returns; it does not guarantee profit or prevent every asset from falling together.

Give each asset a clear job

Equity represents ownership exposure to businesses. It can support long-term growth goals, but prices and earnings can fluctuate substantially. Equity needed for an imminent expense may have to be sold during a market decline. A long horizon improves flexibility but does not turn uncertain returns into a promise.

Debt supplies lending exposure. Credit quality, maturity, interest-rate sensitivity and liquidity matter. A long-duration bond fund can fall when yields rise, and a low-quality issuer can fail to repay. The word debt does not establish that a holding is equivalent to a bank balance.

Gold offers a different economic exposure and does not itself generate business earnings or contractual coupon income. Its rupee return depends on the relevant gold price and currency effects, alongside product costs. Cash supports near-term spending and flexibility, but can lose purchasing power when inflation exceeds its net return.

SEBI's investment guidance links allocation to goals, risk tolerance and time horizon. Treat each holding as a tool for a defined purpose, rather than adding an asset simply because its recent return looks attractive.

Separate risk capacity from comfort

Risk tolerance is how you feel about uncertainty. Risk capacity is the financial ability to endure it. Someone may feel comfortable with equities while having a school-fee payment due in six months; that near-term obligation limits the risk capacity of the money reserved for it.

List goals separately: emergency reserve, known purchases, medium-term commitments and distant retirement. Note the date, essential versus flexible nature, and any dependable income supporting each. This is more useful than applying one portfolio percentage to every rupee the household owns.

Employment exposure also matters. A person whose income and employer shares both depend on one industry may already have substantial economic concentration. Owning several funds concentrated in the same industry does not remove that underlying vulnerability.

Calculate a hypothetical allocation

Suppose a teaching portfolio of ₹10 lakh is divided into ₹5 lakh equity, ₹3 lakh debt, ₹1 lakh gold and ₹1 lakh cash. The weights are 50%, 30%, 10% and 10%. These figures demonstrate calculations; they are not a recommended allocation.

After a hypothetical year, assume equity rises 20%, debt 5%, gold falls 10% and cash earns zero. Values become ₹6 lakh, ₹3.15 lakh, ₹90,000 and ₹1 lakh. Total value is ₹11.05 lakh, a 10.5% portfolio return before investor-specific costs and taxes.

The new equity weight is 600000/1105000, about 54.3%. Allocation drift is a change in risk exposure, even if you made no trades. A portfolio that performed well can become more concentrated in the asset responsible for that performance.

Rebalance using a written rule

Rebalancing restores the intended mix. In the example, a 50% equity target implies ₹5,52,500 of equity, compared with the actual ₹6 lakh. The difference is ₹47,500. You could reduce equity or direct new contributions towards underweight assets, depending on available cash and transaction consequences.

Investor.gov's allocation guide explains how uneven returns change weights and how rebalancing can restore them. Choose a review interval or tolerance band in advance. Constantly responding to headlines can create unnecessary trading and undermine the plan.

Account for applicable taxes, exit loads and costs before selling. Rebalancing is a risk-management process, not evidence that the sold asset is about to fall. Review the target itself when the goal, income stability or household obligations change. A spreadsheet cannot decide these priorities without accurate inputs about the person using it.

Quick questions, clear answers

How many funds create diversification?

There is no universal fund count. Compare actual holdings, sectors, issuers and asset exposures. Ten overlapping equity funds can be less diversified than a small portfolio with complementary roles. Fund names and the number of account lines do not measure economic concentration reliably.

Should cash always be minimised?

No. Cash needed for emergencies or imminent expenses has a practical role even if its expected long-term return is low. Excess cash for distant goals can create inflation risk, but insufficient accessible cash may force borrowing or selling investments at a bad time.

Can I use an age-based allocation formula?

Such formulas can start a discussion but ignore dependants, debt, pensions, employment stability and goal timing. Two people of the same age can have very different financial capacity. Use a goal and cash-flow assessment, then test the resulting allocation against plausible adverse scenarios.
