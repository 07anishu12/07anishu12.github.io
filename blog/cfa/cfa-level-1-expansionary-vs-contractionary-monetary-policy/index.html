Introduction

Expansionary vs Contractionary Monetary Policy Explained is a core Level I Economics topic (LM 4: Monetary Policy — Open market operations, policy rate, reserve requirements, quantitative easing (QE), central bank independence/credibility, liquidity trap) focusing on central bank policy tools (open market operations, policy rate, reserve requirements, QE), qualities of effective central banks, and limitations (liquidity trap, bond vigilantes). This guide covers the governing equations (Fractional Reserve Deposit Creation & Money Supply and Real Interest Rate & Zero Lower Bound (Liquidity Trap)), two verified numerical examples, a comparison table, and three original CFA-style practice questions.

Concept Explanation & Institutional Framework

From first principles, central bank policy tools (open market operations, policy rate, reserve requirements, QE), qualities of effective central banks, and limitations (liquidity trap, bond vigilantes) links theoretical valuation and risk mechanics to practical decisions in Economics. Fractional Reserve Deposit Creation & Money Supply (Max New Deposits = Initial Excess Reserves × (1 / Reserve Requirement Ratio)) establishes the primary relationship (Buying bonds in an open market operation injects reserves into commercial banks, expanding broad money through fractional reserve lending.), while Real Interest Rate & Zero Lower Bound (Liquidity Trap) (Real Rate = Nominal Policy Rate − Expected Inflation) governs When nominal rates hit 0% and deflation sets in, real rates remain high and money demand becomes infinitely elastic (liquidity trap).

Step-by-Step Worked Numerical Examples

Worked Example 1: Open Market Purchase and Fractional Reserve Money Multiplier

Problem & Given Scenario: To stimulate a slowing economy, the central bank purchases $5.0 billion of government bonds from commercial banks via an Open Market Operation (OMO). The required reserve ratio is r_req = 8.0%, and banks hold zero excess reserves and zero cash drain.

Formula Expression
Max New Deposits = Initial Excess Reserves × (1 / Reserve Requirement Ratio)

Step-by-Step Substitution & Calculation:

Money Multiplier = 1 / 0.08 = 12.50x. Maximum potential expansion in total bank deposits (money supply) = $5.0B × 12.50 = +$62.50 billion (consisting of the initial $5.0B injection plus $57.5B of secondary loan creation).

Tip: Financial Interpretation & Exam Takeaway: Buying bonds injects bank reserves (expansionary); selling bonds drains bank reserves (contractionary).

Worked Example 2: Diagnosing a Deflationary Liquidity Trap vs. Quantitative Easing (QE)

Problem & Given Scenario: During a severe recession, nominal short-term policy rates are cut to 0.00%, but deflation of −1.50% per year persists. Compare the real short-term rate before and after a Quantitative Easing (QE) program that restores inflation expectations to +1.75%.

Formula Expression
Real Rate = Nominal Policy Rate − Expected Inflation

Step-by-Step Substitution & Calculation:

Before QE (in deflation): Real Rate = 0.00% − (−1.50%) = +1.50% (punitive real borrowing cost despite 0% nominal rates!). After QE purchases of long-term bonds restore +1.75% inflation expectations: Real Rate = 0.00% − (+1.75%) = −1.75% (325 bps reduction in real rates).

Tip: Financial Interpretation & Exam Takeaway: When conventional policy hits the zero lower bound (liquidity trap), central banks deploy Quantitative Easing (buying long-duration government/MBS bonds) to flatten long-term yields and lift inflation expectations.

CFA Practice Questions & Detailed Solutions

Practice Question 1

Question: To implement a contractionary monetary policy using open market operations, a central bank will:

  • [A] Buy government bonds from commercial banks, increasing bank reserves

  • [B] Sell government bonds to commercial banks, draining bank reserves and raising interbank overnight lending rates

  • [C] Lower the discount rate and reserve requirement

Tip: Correct Answer: Option B.

Explanation: Selling government securities absorbs cash reserves from the banking system, contracting liquidity and pushing short-term interest rates higher.

Practice Question 2

Question: In the CFA curriculum, the three essential qualities of an effective, credible central bank are:

  • [A] Independence (operational and target), Credibility, and Transparency

  • [B] Subordination to the Ministry of Finance, Secrecy, and Fixed Exchange Rates

  • [C] Zero inflation targets, Gold standard backing, and Balanced budgets

Tip: Correct Answer: Option A.

Explanation: Independence (especially operational independence to set rates free of political interference), credibility, and transparency anchor public inflation expectations.

Practice Question 3

Question: A 'liquidity trap' severely limits the effectiveness of conventional expansionary monetary policy because:

  • [A] Commercial banks face a 100% reserve requirement

  • [B] Nominal interest rates are near zero, deflationary expectations prevail, and the public hoards any injected cash rather than spending or borrowing

  • [C] The government is running a fiscal surplus

Tip: Correct Answer: Option B.

Explanation: In a liquidity trap, money demand becomes infinitely elastic at near-zero nominal rates: injecting money into banks fails to stimulate lending or aggregate demand.

Common Mistakes & Exam Traps to Avoid

  • Timing Errors: Mixing beginning and ending balances in Real Interest Rate & Zero Lower Bound (Liquidity Trap).
