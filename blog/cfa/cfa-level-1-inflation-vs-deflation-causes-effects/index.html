Formula 1: Laspeyres (Fixed Base Basket) vs. Paasche (Current Basket) Price Index

Formula Expression
I_Laspeyres = [∑ (P_t × Q_0) / ∑ (P_0 × Q_0)] × 100

I_Paasche = [∑ (P_t × Q_t) / ∑ (P_0 × Q_t)] × 100

Institutional Context & Variable Breakdown:

  • Laspeyres holds base-period quantities Q_0 fixed (creating substitution bias).

  • Paasche uses current quantities Q_t.

  • Fisher Index = √(I_Laspeyres × I_Paasche).

Formula Expression
Unit Labor Cost (ULC) = Total Hourly Labor Compensation / Output per Hour (Productivity)

Institutional Context & Variable Breakdown:

  • Core inflation strips out volatile food and energy.

Step-by-Step Worked Numerical Examples

Worked Example 1: Demonstrating Substitution Bias: Laspeyres vs. Paasche vs. Fisher Index

Problem & Given Scenario: In Period 0, households buy 100 lb of Beef @ $10 ($1,000) and 100 lb of Chicken @ $5 ($500), so Base Cost = $1,500. In Period 1, Beef price jumps to $16 while Chicken stays $5; consumers substitute toward chicken, buying 60 lb of Beef and 140 lb of Chicken.

Formula Expression
I_Laspeyres = [∑ (P_t × Q_0) / ∑ (P_0 × Q_0)] × 100

I_Paasche = [∑ (P_t × Q_t) / ∑ (P_0 × Q_t)] × 100

Step-by-Step Substitution & Calculation:

  • Step 1. Laspeyres Index (holds Q_0 = 100, 100 fixed): Cost_1(Q_0) = 100($16) + 100($5) = $2,100; I_L = ($2,100 / $1,500) × 100 = 140.00 (+40.0% inflation).

  • Step 2. Paasche Index (uses Q_1 = 60, 140): Cost_1(Q_1) = 60($16) + 140($5) = $1,660; Cost_0(Q_1) = 60($10) + 140($5) = $1,300; I_P = ($1,660 / $1,300) × 100 = 127.69 (+27.69% inflation).

  • Step 3. Fisher Ideal Index = √(140.00 × 127.6923) = 133.70 (+33.70% inflation).

Tip: Financial Interpretation & Exam Takeaway: Because a Laspeyres index ignores consumers switching from expensive beef to cheaper chicken, it overstates true cost-of-living inflation (+40.0% vs +33.7% Fisher).

Worked Example 2: Unanticipated Inflation vs. Deflation Wealth Transfer Between Borrowers and Lenders

Problem & Given Scenario: A corporation issues a 10-year fixed-rate bond at a nominal coupon of 5.0% when expected inflation is 2.0% (expected real rate = 3.0%). Compare the realized real interest burden if actual inflation averages (A) +6.0% (unanticipated inflation) vs. (B) −1.5% (deflation).

Formula Expression
Unit Labor Cost (ULC) = Total Hourly Labor Compensation / Output per Hour (Productivity)

Step-by-Step Substitution & Calculation:

Case A (6.0% inflation): Real interest cost ≈ 5.0% − 6.0% = −1.0% (borrower repays in depreciated dollars; wealth transfers from lender to borrower). Case B (−1.5% deflation): Real interest cost ≈ 5.0% − (−1.5%) = +6.5% (real debt burden surges; wealth transfers from borrower to lender, risking defaults).

Tip: Financial Interpretation & Exam Takeaway: Deflation is particularly dangerous to highly leveraged economies because nominal debt contracts cannot adjust downward when prices and wages fall.

CFA Practice Questions & Detailed Solutions

Practice Question 1

Question: Which of the following is NOT one of the three upward biases inherent in a fixed-basket Laspeyres Consumer Price Index (CPI)?

  • [A] Substitution bias (consumers shift away from relatively more expensive goods)

  • [B] Quality improvement bias and new product introduction bias

  • [C] Downward import weight bias

Tip: Correct Answer: Option C.

Explanation: A fixed-basket Laspeyres CPI suffers from three classic upward biases: substitution bias, quality (hedonic) improvement bias, and new product/outlet bias, causing it to overstate true cost-of-living inflation by ~0.5%–1.0% annually.

Practice Question 2

Question: If an economy's annual inflation rate declines from 7.5% in 2024 to 4.2% in 2025 and 2.5% in 2026, the economy is experiencing:

  • [A] Deflation

  • [B] Disinflation

  • [C] Hyperinflation

Tip: Correct Answer: Option B.

Explanation: Disinflation is a deceleration in the positive rate of inflation (the price level continues to rise, but at a slower percentage pace), whereas deflation requires a negative inflation rate (< 0%).

Practice Question 3

Question: A sudden sharp increase in global energy prices and unit labor costs that shifts the Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS) curve to the left results in:

  • [A] Demand-pull inflation with falling unemployment

  • [B] Cost-push inflation (stagflation), characterized by a higher price level and lower real GDP

  • [C] Deflation with rising real GDP

Tip: Correct Answer: Option B.

Explanation: A leftward shift in SRAS raises the aggregate price level while simultaneously contracting real GDP and raising unemployment (stagflation).
