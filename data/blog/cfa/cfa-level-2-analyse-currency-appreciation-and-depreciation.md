## Core Formulas & Mathematical Equations

> [!NOTE]
> The following analytical equations govern problem-solving and numerical questions across this curriculum reading.

### Formula 1: Dornbusch Monetary Overshooting Model vs. Pure Monetary Model

```formula
Pure Monetary: Flexible Prices => +ΔM% causes Immediate +ΔP% and Immediate −ΔCurrency%

Dornbusch: Sticky Short-Run Goods Prices => +ΔM% lowers real rates, causing Currency to OVERSHOOT its long-run PPP depreciation in the short run!
```

**Institutional Context & Variable Breakdown:** Because goods prices adjust slowly in the short run while FX/financial markets adjust instantaneously, an unexpected money supply expansion drops domestic interest rates and forces the currency to depreciate BEYOND its long-run PPP level initially, then appreciate back toward PPP as prices rise.

### Formula 2: Mundell-Fleming Model (High Capital Mobility — Developed Markets)

```formula
Expansionary Monetary Policy => Lower r => Capital Outflow => Currency Depreciates

Expansionary Fiscal Policy => Higher r => Capital Inflow => Currency Appreciates!
```

**Institutional Context & Variable Breakdown:** Under high capital mobility, financial capital flows dominate trade flows in the short run: the policy that raises interest rates (Expansionary Fiscal or Tight Monetary) appreciates the currency.

## Step-by-Step Worked Numerical Examples

### Worked Example 1: Applying the Mundell-Fleming Model Under High vs. Low Capital Mobility

**Problem & Given Scenario:**
Compare the short-run impact on a country's floating currency under (A) High Capital Mobility vs. (B) Low Capital Mobility when the government implements an Expansionary Fiscal Policy alongside a Contractionary Monetary Policy vs. Expansionary Fiscal + Expansionary Monetary Policy.

```formula
Pure Monetary: Flexible Prices => +ΔM% causes Immediate +ΔP% and Immediate −ΔCurrency%

Dornbusch: Sticky Short-Run Goods Prices => +ΔM% lowers real rates, causing Currency to OVERSHOOT its long-run PPP depreciation in the short run!
```

**Step-by-Step Substitution & Calculation:**

- **Step 1. Expansionary Fiscal + Contractionary Monetary under HIGH Capital Mobility: Both policies push domestic interest rates sharply higher -> Massive capital inflow -> Domestic currency APPRECIATES unambiguously!**

- **Step 2. Expansionary Fiscal under LOW Capital Mobility (capital controls): Trade flows dominate capital flows -> Higher GDP increases imports (worsening the trade balance) -> Domestic currency DEPRECIATES!**

- **Step 3. Expansionary Fiscal + Expansionary Monetary under High Capital Mobility: Fiscal pushes r up (appreciation) while Monetary pushes r down (depreciation) -> Indeterminate / Ambiguous exchange rate impact!**

> [!TIP]
> **Financial Interpretation & Exam Takeaway:**
> Notice the flip for Expansionary Fiscal Policy: under HIGH capital mobility it APPRECIATES the currency (via higher interest rates attracting capital inflows), whereas under LOW capital mobility it DEPRECIATES the currency (via higher imports worsening the trade balance)!

### Worked Example 2: Tracing the Long-Run Portfolio Balance (Asset Market) Model of Fiscal Deficits

**Problem & Given Scenario:**
While Mundell-Fleming shows that an Expansionary Fiscal deficit appreciates the currency in the short run (via higher interest rates), what happens in the LONG RUN under the Portfolio Balance Model?

```formula
Expansionary Monetary Policy => Lower r => Capital Outflow => Currency Depreciates

Expansionary Fiscal Policy => Higher r => Capital Inflow => Currency Appreciates!
```

**Step-by-Step Substitution & Calculation:**

In the Long Run, sustained fiscal deficits accumulate into a massive stock of sovereign debt. Foreign investors demand a higher sovereign risk premium (or central bank debt monetization fears rise) to hold the growing share of domestic bonds in their global portfolios, causing the currency to DEPRECIATE in the long run!

> [!TIP]
> **Financial Interpretation & Exam Takeaway:**
> Combining Mundell-Fleming (short-run appreciation) with the Portfolio Balance Model (long-run debt-stock depreciation) shows that sustained fiscal deficits cause a currency to appreciate initially and then depreciate in the long run.

## Comparison & Reference Analysis

| Policy Mix (Floating FX) | High Capital Mobility Impact | Low Capital Mobility Impact | Driving Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expansionary Monetary | Depreciates (Lower r + Higher Imports) | Depreciates (Higher GDP -> Imports) | Unambiguously Depreciates in both! |
| Contractionary Monetary | Appreciates (Higher r + Lower Imports) | Appreciates (Lower GDP -> Less Imports) | Unambiguously Appreciates in both! |
| Expansionary Fiscal | Appreciates (Higher r attracts capital) | Depreciates (Higher GDP boosts imports) | Capital flows vs Trade flows flip! |
| Contractionary Fiscal | Depreciates (Lower r -> capital outflow) | Appreciates (Lower GDP cuts imports) | Capital flows vs Trade flows flip! |
| Tight Monetary + Easy Fiscal | Strongly Appreciates (Both raise r) | Ambiguous (Trade effects oppose) | Maximum short-run currency strength |

## CFA Practice Questions & Detailed Solutions

### Practice Question 1

**Question:** According to the Mundell-Fleming model for a developed economy with a floating exchange rate and HIGH capital mobility, what is the short-run effect on the domestic currency of an expansionary fiscal policy combined with a contractionary monetary policy?

- **[A]** The domestic currency will depreciate

- **[B]** The domestic currency will appreciate, because both expansionary fiscal policy and contractionary monetary policy increase domestic interest rates, attracting foreign capital inflows

- **[C]** The effect on the currency is indeterminate

> [!TIP]
> **Correct Answer: Option B.**
>
> **Explanation:** Under high capital mobility, capital account flows dominate: expansionary fiscal policy increases credit demand (raising r) and contractionary monetary policy reduces money supply (raising r), so both policies reinforce capital inflows and appreciate the currency.

### Practice Question 2

**Question:** In Rudiger Dornbusch's monetary 'overshooting' model, why does an unexpected permanent 5% increase in the domestic money supply cause the domestic currency to depreciate by MORE than 5% in the short run?

- **[A]** Because goods prices are completely flexible in the short run

- **[B]** Because domestic goods prices are sticky (inflexible) in the short run, so the nominal money increase increases real money balances and drives domestic interest rates down—requiring the currency to overshoot its long-run 5% PPP depreciation initially so that expected future appreciation compensates for the lower domestic interest rate

- **[C]** Because the central bank intervenes in the FX market

> [!TIP]
> **Correct Answer: Option B.**
>
> **Explanation:** With sticky short-run prices, the real money supply rises and domestic interest rates fall; by UIRP, for investors to hold lower-yielding domestic bonds, the currency must drop below its long-run PPP value today so it is expected to appreciate back up toward PPP as prices gradually rise.

### Practice Question 3

**Question:** How does a persistent Current Account Surplus affect a country's currency over the long run?

- **[A]** It places upward (appreciating) pressure on the currency through the flow supply/demand channel, accumulation of net foreign assets (wealth transfer), and reduced external debt vulnerability

- **[B]** It causes the currency to depreciate

- **[C]** It has zero effect on exchange rates

> [!TIP]
> **Correct Answer: Option A.**
>
> **Explanation:** Persistent current account surpluses transfer wealth from deficit nations to surplus nations and require foreign buyers to purchase the surplus country's currency, driving long-run appreciation.
