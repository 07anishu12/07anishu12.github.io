An electric car makes financial sense when its lifetime operating savings exceed its extra purchase and financing costs. There is no universal monthly mileage threshold for India. Your charging access, local tariffs, comparable vehicles and ownership period determine the answer; this guide shows a reusable calculation with explicitly hypothetical inputs.

## Compare equivalent vehicles and complete prices

Start with cars that provide similar passenger space, transmission convenience and essential equipment. Comparing a basic petrol hatchback with a premium electric SUV can answer which costs less, but not whether the powertrain itself caused the difference.

Get itemised on-road quotations for both. Include charger installation, registration charges that actually apply, insurance and any required electrical work. Do not assume a national or state EV incentive remains available or applies to your purchase without checking the current rules and invoice.

The [Tata.ev portfolio](https://ev.tata.cars/) explicitly identifies advertised prices as ex-showroom and subject to change. That illustrates why an online headline is not the final acquisition cost. Record location, quotation date, variant and whether a battery or charging arrangement has separate commercial terms.

## Calculate petrol and electricity costs correctly

Petrol cost per kilometre equals petrol price per litre divided by kilometres per litre. Electric cost per kilometre equals wall electricity consumed per kilometre multiplied by the electricity tariff per kWh. Wall consumption includes charging losses; battery-display consumption may not.

For a wholly hypothetical example, petrol is ₹100/litre and economy is 15 km/l. Cost is ₹100 ÷ 15 = ₹6.67/km. An EV consuming 0.16 kWh/km from the wall at ₹9/kWh costs ₹1.44/km. The assumed energy saving is ₹5.23/km.

Replace them with your bills and conservative comparable consumption evidence. For public charging, include the actual tariff structure, session fees and any parking charge attributable to charging.

If 70% of your energy costs ₹9/kWh at home and 30% costs ₹20/kWh publicly, the weighted tariff is ₹12.30/kWh. At the same assumed wall consumption, electricity costs ₹1.97/km, reducing the energy saving to approximately ₹4.70/km.

## Convert the premium into a distance threshold

Suppose the complete EV purchase costs ₹3,00,000 more than the comparable petrol car. Using the unrounded saving of ₹5.2266667/km, simple energy-only break-even is ₹3,00,000 ÷ ₹5.23, or approximately 57,398 km.

Over a five-year holding period, that is around 11,480 km annually, or 957 km monthly. This is an illustrative result, not a recommendation that every Indian buyer crossing 957 km should choose an EV. Different prices or charging patterns produce a different threshold.

A more complete formula is: required distance = net fixed premium ÷ net variable saving per kilometre. Net fixed premium includes extra finance, installation and other fixed differences, less any defensible resale advantage. Keep uncertain future resale separate in scenarios rather than assuming it.

## Add the expenses that energy comparisons miss

Compare insurance with equivalent cover and deductibles. The [IRDAI consumer guide](https://irdai.gov.in/web/policy-holder/motor-insurance) explains why IDV and coverage must be considered alongside premium. Ask how the policy treats battery damage and relevant charging equipment; do not infer coverage from the word comprehensive.

Include scheduled service, tyres and repair reserves based on the specific vehicles. An EV does not require every combustion-engine maintenance task, but that does not mean maintenance or accident repairs cost nothing. Obtain written schedules and prices where possible.

For financing, compare total repayment rather than just EMI. A larger loan or longer tenure can consume part of the energy saving. Avoid counting both the full purchase price and all loan principal repayments as separate costs in the same total-cost model.

## Test convenience and uncertainty before deciding

Home charging can turn a daily task into an overnight routine, provided the parking and electrical arrangements are workable. If you rely on public charging, budget both money and time, verify compatible working sites and plan backups for regular journeys.

Run low-, normal- and high-distance scenarios. Then raise electricity cost, lower petrol economy advantage or change resale assumptions. If the decision reverses easily, recognise that financial superiority is uncertain and choose according to practical needs and risk tolerance.

Prefer an EV when charging is reliable, the conservative model supports the premium and the vehicle suits your trips. Prefer petrol when acquisition cost, low usage or charging inconvenience makes the EV case weak. Neither conclusion needs an invented nationwide threshold.

## Quick questions, clear answers

### Should I count a battery replacement after five years?

Do not automatically assume one. Use the actual warranty, condition expectations and credible model-specific evidence. Show a separate repair-risk scenario if concerned, rather than treating an unsupported replacement bill as inevitable or pretending the battery has no financial risk.

### Can free public charging change the result?

Yes, while the offer actually applies. Verify duration, eligible vehicle, network and usage limits. Calculate a second case after the promotion ends. A temporary benefit should not be projected across an entire ownership period without contractual support.

### Does lower EMI mean lower ownership cost?

No. Tenure, down payment, fees and interest can produce a low monthly figure with a higher total payment. Compare cash outflow and total cost separately, using the same holding period and realistic residual value assumptions for both cars.
