Before paying for a used car, verify who owns it, whether finance remains recorded, whether its history is credible and whether an independent inspection supports the asking price.  Treat document checks and mechanical inspection as parallel requirements, with payment tied to an agreed transfer process.

## Establish identity and the seller's authority

Match the registration certificate to the vehicle's registration, chassis and engine identifiers using the appropriate records and physical markings. Ask why the seller's name differs if an intermediary is involved. A broker's possession of keys does not establish the right to sell.

Check registration status, fuel type, model and relevant validity information through official channels. Confirm any local restrictions that affect the vehicle's use, especially when buying across states or considering an older car. Do not assume a car can be driven indefinitely everywhere because its RC is available.

Inspect the insurance schedule and ask about previous claims. Policy validity is not proof that every ownership or coverage change has been completed. Obtain the insurer's specific transfer instructions and confirm the required process for your transaction.

## Resolve loans and ownership-transfer documents

The [Parivahan ownership guidance](https://mparivahan.parivahan.gov.in/mstatic/english/rc-info-ownership.html) identifies Forms 29 and 30 for ordinary ownership-transfer steps and Form 35 for termination of hypothecation. Actual filing requirements, fees and supporting documents can vary with the transaction and state.

If the RC records a financier, establish the outstanding loan and the route to clearing it. [Parivahan's hypothecation guidance](https://mparivahan.parivahan.gov.in/mstatic/english/rc-info-hp-termination.html) describes financier clearance and Form 35 in the termination process. A seller saying the loan is paid is not the same as verifying the record and necessary documents.

Use a documented payment arrangement agreed with the relevant parties. Avoid paying the entire price on a verbal promise that a lender NOC or transfer paperwork will appear later. Cross-state purchases, inherited vehicles and company-owned cars can require additional steps; verify these before committing.

## Read history as a connected record

Ask for dated service invoices, not just a stamped booklet. Compare kilometres and dates across workshop records, insurance documents and available inspection reports. A low odometer reading with missing records deserves investigation, but a single gap is not by itself proof of tampering.

With the owner's consent, ask the authorised workshop what history it can legitimately disclose. Look for repeated overheating, major engine work, flood-related repairs or recurring electronic faults. Written records can identify questions that a cosmetic inspection misses.

Separate normal wear from a pattern of deferred maintenance. New tyres do not prove the car is healthy, and a recent service does not establish that every outstanding issue was repaired. Ask for estimates for work likely to fall due soon.

## Arrange an independent inspection

Choose a qualified inspector or workshop independent of the seller. Request checks appropriate to the model: structure and accident repairs, corrosion, leaks, cooling system, brakes, suspension, tyres, electrical systems and diagnostic fault information. A scan can support assessment but does not guarantee there are no hidden faults.

Inspect in daylight and examine the car from cold if the workshop considers that useful. Test every promised function and compare the keys and accessories with the listing. Avoid interpreting superficial paint differences as a definitive diagnosis without professional assessment.

For an EV, add manufacturer-supported battery-health and charging checks. For a modified vehicle, confirm the approval, insurance and warranty implications. An inspector should identify limitations and inaccessible areas instead of issuing an unrealistic guarantee of future reliability.

## Price the risk and organise handover

Consider a hypothetical car advertised at ₹5 lakh. Inspection identifies ₹25,000 of immediate work and you allow a ₹20,000 contingency. Your effective initial budget becomes ₹5.45 lakh before transfer, insurance and financing costs. This is an illustrative purchase model, not a market valuation.

Compare that total with real alternatives rather than negotiating only the headline price. A cheaper car with serious unresolved structural or documentation problems is not necessarily a bargain. Decide in advance which findings mean walking away, particularly unclear ownership or unacceptable safety-related repairs.

Record the agreed price, vehicle identifiers, odometer reading, handover date, included items and responsibility for the transfer. Keep payment evidence and submission acknowledgements. Follow up until the official ownership and relevant insurance records reflect the completed transaction; possession alone does not finish the process.

## Quick questions, clear answers

### Is a dealer inspection certificate enough?

It can be useful evidence, but read its scope, exclusions and commercial terms. An independent inspection helps when the seller controls the assessment. Neither document guarantees future reliability, and unresolved title or finance issues still need separate verification before payment.

### Can I buy a car with an outstanding loan?

Possibly, through a properly documented settlement and transfer arrangement involving the financier where necessary. Establish the balance, release conditions and record-update process first. Do not assume the seller's verbal assurance or an old payment receipt removes recorded hypothecation.

### Should a missing service history always stop the purchase?

It increases uncertainty rather than proving a defect. Ask whether reliable records can be recovered and what inspection can establish. Price any remaining risk realistically; for a complex or expensive vehicle, weak history may be enough reason to choose a better-documented alternative.
