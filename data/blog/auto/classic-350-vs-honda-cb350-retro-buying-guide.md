The Classic 350 and Honda CB350 should be compared as exact current models, not as vague brand reputations. The inspected Classic page documents a 349 cc engine and several wheel/tyre arrangements; Honda's exact CB350 page describes HSTC, a split seat and assist/slipper clutch. Distinguish CB350 from H'ness, CB350RS and CB350C before making a specification table.

## Establish the identities and supported hardware

The [Classic 350 specification page](https://www.royalenfield.com/in/en/app/motorcycles/classic-350/) lists 349 cc, 20.2 bhp and 27 Nm for the configuration described. The [Honda CB350 page](https://www.honda2wheelersindia.com/motorcycle/CB350) identifies the exact model separately from other CB350-family products. Do not borrow an H'ness engine or feature table without confirming applicability.

The Classic source gives an 805 mm seat height and 195 kg kerb weight under its stated measurement basis. Those numbers are reasons to test reach and parking effort; they do not establish that every rider will find it difficult or that an unmeasured Honda is easier.

The inspected Honda page describes a 310 mm front disc, while the Classic source lists a 300 mm front disc. Diameter alone does not rank braking distance. Tyres, control systems, surface, load and rider input all affect the result, and no braking test was performed here.

## Compare safety and tyre arrangements specifically

Honda describes Selectable Torque Control on the CB350 page. It is a documented reason to examine that model if rear-wheel torque intervention is a priority, but its presence does not eliminate the need for careful throttle use. Read the manual's conditions and operation.

Royal Enfield's inspected specification distinguishes spoke-wheel tube-type and alloy-wheel tubeless tyre combinations. That is an important practical variant difference within the Classic range. Confirm your actual quote rather than assuming every Classic uses the same puncture-repair approach.

Check ABS coverage on both selected variants in the current brochure and demonstrate warning-light behaviour. Do not assume a traction-control feature and ABS perform the same job. A tyre or brake upgrade should remain compatible with manufacturer requirements rather than being selected solely for appearance.

## Decide which retro layout fits your body

The Honda source describes a split seat and assist/slipper clutch; the Classic source also describes its current clutch and seating-related equipment. Availability should be checked for the ordered trims. A shared feature name does not establish equal lever effort or seat comfort.

Try both in riding gear. Check wrist and shoulder position, knee bend and whether you can manoeuvre the motorcycle backwards on level ground. If daily parking includes a slope, consider the actual movement required rather than relying on seat height alone.

Bring the regular pillion and check mounting, footrests and grab support. A visually comfortable broad saddle may still create pressure or awkward posture for a particular person. No first-hand comfort ranking is claimed in this guide.

## Check the local ownership arrangement

Confirm the appropriate Honda sales/service channel for the exact CB350 and identify the authorised workshop you would use. Do the same for the Classic. Distance, appointments and parts-ordering processes matter more than an unsupported statement that one brand always has better service.

Ask for scheduled maintenance, consumables, tyres and common wear-item estimates over the same annual distance. Follow the correct oil and service specifications; the requirements for one retro single should not be applied to another by engine-class label.

For touring, inspect luggage compatibility, permitted load and the practicality of repair support along your usual routes. Avoid assuming a reputation for reliability proves an individual vehicle cannot fail. This research establishes no controlled comparative failure-rate evidence.

## Compare the premium with a realistic budget

Suppose a hypothetical preferred variant costs ₹25,000 more. Over five years, that is roughly ₹417 monthly before interest, insurance and resale. Decide whether its actual equipment, tyre arrangement or better demonstrated fit warrants that amount.

An assumed 1,000 km monthly at ₹100/litre costs about ₹3,333 at 30 km/l and ₹3,125 at 32 km/l. The difference is about ₹208 monthly. These are illustrative, not Classic or CB350 economy results, and should not be used as evidence of a fuel-saving winner.

Prioritise CB350 when its verified torque-control package and exact configuration address your needs. Prioritise Classic when its documented variant choices and your tested fit provide the preferred ownership package. Reconcile model year, wheels, braking and warranty before booking; neither name guarantees a superior personal experience.

## Quick questions, clear answers

### Are CB350, H'ness and CB350RS the same model?

Honda lists them separately. Related branding does not mean identical seating, equipment or specification. Use the exact product page and brochure corresponding to the invoice, and identify any comparison that intentionally uses a different derivative.

### Are all Classic 350 tyres tubeless?

The inspected specification distinguishes tube-type spoke-wheel and tubeless alloy-wheel arrangements. Confirm the chosen variant's approved setup. Do not fit a tubeless tyre to an incompatible wheel or assume appearance alone reveals the correct repair method.

### Does the larger front disc guarantee better stopping?

No. Diameter is one hardware detail, not a measured braking outcome. Compare complete brake systems, tyre condition and ABS coverage, then practise correct braking in a suitable setting. No stopping-distance ranking was established for these motorcycles here.
