The P/E ratio compares a share's price with earnings per share. A high P/E may reflect strong expected growth, lower perceived risk or temporary weak earnings; a low P/E may reflect a bargain, weak prospects or unusually high profits. The number is a starting question, not an investment conclusion, and negative earnings make ordinary P/E comparisons difficult.

## Calculate the ratio on a stated basis

Use `P/E = Share price / Earnings per share`. A hypothetical price ₹300 and EPS ₹15 imply 20×. Equivalently, use consistent ordinary equity value divided by earnings attributable to those shareholders. Check dilution and the earnings definition before assuming the two approaches will match exactly.

Trailing P/E uses a recent reported earnings period, commonly the last twelve months. Forward P/E uses forecast earnings for a stated future period. [CFA Institute's multiples reading](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/market-based-valuation-price-enterprise-value-multiples?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_little-text-block) distinguishes these bases and discusses normalising volatile earnings.

Do not compare a trailing ratio for one company with a forward ratio for another without explaining the difference. A forecast can be useful but is not an observed fact. Record the price date, EPS period, source and any adjustments beside the calculation.

## Understand what the market may be pricing

A higher multiple can reflect expectations of future earnings growth or more dependable earnings. It can also reflect excessive optimism. A lower multiple can reflect higher risk, slower growth, poor governance, debt pressure or a business facing structural decline.

Suppose two synthetic companies each have EPS ₹10. One trades at ₹100, or 10×, and the other at ₹200, or 20×. The first is cheaper per current rupee of earnings, but that does not establish better value. Future earnings, required reinvestment, risk and shareholder distributions may differ substantially.

A company growing earnings must often retain cash or raise capital to fund that growth. Compare growth quality rather than assuming a higher percentage is automatically worth a higher multiple. An acquisition-funded earnings increase can also carry debt, dilution and integration risks.

## Watch the denominator through the cycle

A cyclical company may look cheapest when profits are near a temporary peak. If hypothetical price is ₹200 and peak EPS ₹40, P/E is 5×. If sustainable EPS is instead ₹10, the same price corresponds to 20× normalised earnings. The low observed multiple may be warning about the peak rather than indicating a straightforward bargain.

One-off gains can also inflate EPS. Inspect asset sales, tax credits, fair-value changes and adjustments. Conversely, a genuine temporary expense can depress earnings, but repeatedly excluding recurring costs can make “adjusted” EPS too generous.

For losses, a negative P/E is not meaningfully ranked like an ordinary positive ratio. Consider the path to sustainable profitability and another suitable valuation framework. Revenue multiples can be used in some cases, but they bring their own dependence on margins and do not make loss-making businesses easy to value.

## Build a comparison that answers the question

Use peers with similar business models, accounting, geography and periods. Differences in leverage and tax affect net income, so P/E is not financing-neutral. Review EV-based measures and cash flow where they help explain apparent discrepancies.

Calculate earnings yield as `EPS/Price`, the reciprocal of positive P/E. A 20× P/E implies a 5% earnings yield, but this is not a guaranteed cash distribution or bond yield. Earnings may be retained, reinvested poorly or revised; capital value can fall.

In a practical worksheet, show price, EPS, P/E, growth, net debt, return on capital and cash conversion. Add reported versus normalised earnings where justified. A useful conclusion explains the conditions under which the current price is reasonable rather than announcing that every ratio below a chosen threshold is cheap.

No current stock price, sector-average multiple or return forecast is asserted here. All numbers are synthetic educational examples. Before relying on a live ratio, rebuild the earnings base from current filings and examine what expectations would need to come true for the investment to work.

## Quick questions, clear answers

### Does a P/E of ten mean I recover my money in ten years?

Not necessarily. Earnings are not automatically distributed, and future profits can change. The ratio compares current price with one earnings basis; it is not a contractual repayment schedule. Cash distributions, reinvestment outcomes and eventual sale value determine the investor's actual experience.

### Is forward P/E better than trailing P/E?

It can better reflect expected changes, but depends on forecast accuracy. Trailing earnings are more observable yet can be unrepresentative of future conditions. Compare both where useful, identify the assumptions and investigate large differences rather than choosing whichever makes the share look cheaper.

### Can I use one P/E threshold across sectors?

That is usually too crude. Business risk, growth, accounting, capital intensity and earnings stability vary. Even within a sector, company differences matter. Use relevant peers and a reasoned earnings outlook instead of treating one universal threshold as a reliable valuation rule.
