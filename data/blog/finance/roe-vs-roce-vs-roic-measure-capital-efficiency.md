ROE measures profit relative to shareholders' equity; ROCE relates operating profit to capital employed; ROIC relates after-tax operating profit to invested operating capital. Each answers a different question. Define the numerator and denominator before comparing figures, because published conventions vary. A high ratio can reflect strong economics, leverage or a distorted accounting base.

## Match the return to the capital provider

A common ROE formula is `Net income attributable to ordinary shareholders / Average ordinary equity`. The numerator is after financing costs, matching the residual shareholder claim. Use average capital where profit covers a full period, particularly if the balance sheet changes materially during that period.

ROCE is often `EBIT / Capital employed`, where capital employed may be total assets minus current liabilities, or equity plus relevant long-term liabilities under a stated convention. [ACCA's ratio-analysis guide](https://www.accaglobal.com/gb/en/student/exam-support-resources/fundamentals-exams-study-resources/f2/technical-articles/ratio-analysis.html) explains why operating profit should be compared with the corresponding capital base.

ROIC is commonly `NOPAT / Average invested capital`. NOPAT means operating profit after a consistent operating-tax adjustment. Invested capital focuses on the capital supporting operations, with a defined treatment of cash and other non-operating assets. It is not a universally identical data-provider field.

## Calculate a hypothetical comparison

Assume synthetic annual EBIT ₹120 crore, interest ₹20 crore and a 25% illustrative tax rate. Net income is `(120-20)*(1-25%)`, or ₹75 crore. With average ordinary equity ₹500 crore, ROE is 15%.

If average capital employed is ₹800 crore under the chosen definition, ROCE is `120/800`, or 15%. If NOPAT is `120*(1-25%)`, or ₹90 crore, and average invested operating capital is ₹750 crore, ROIC is 12%.

The equal ROE and ROCE in this example is coincidental. The figures use different earnings and capital definitions. The tax assumption is synthetic, not a verified current Indian statutory rate. Record every adjustment rather than inserting one tax percentage into all companies without checking applicability.

In Excel, build separate rows for reported earnings, adjustments, average capital and the final ratios. Confirm that both beginning and ending balances belong to the correct reporting period. Using only closing capital can distort a year containing a large acquisition or equity issue.

## Investigate what makes a ratio high

Leverage can increase ROE when operating returns exceed financing costs, but it also increases financial risk. A company with very little book equity may show a striking ROE that is unstable or economically difficult to interpret. Negative equity can make the ordinary ratio misleading.

Asset impairments can reduce the capital denominator, increasing future reported returns without improving the underlying cash generation. Acquisitions and goodwill can produce the opposite comparability problem. Analyse whether differences reflect operating performance or accounting history.

[CFA Institute's financial-analysis reading](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/financial-analysis-techniques) places profitability ratios alongside liquidity, activity and solvency measures. That broader view helps avoid calling a highly leveraged company superior based solely on one return metric.

Compare like businesses and inspect margins and capital turnover. A retailer can earn an acceptable return through low margins and rapid turnover, while another business requires high margins to compensate for a large capital base. The ratio should lead to an explanation of economics rather than replace it.

## Use ROIC with valuation discipline

Comparing ROIC with WACC can help assess whether operating returns exceed an estimated financing opportunity cost. Align taxes, capital definitions and period assumptions. Book-based historical ROIC and a forward-looking market-based WACC are not perfectly interchangeable measurements.

Growth creates value only under suitable economics. Expanding a low-return business can consume capital without improving shareholder value. Distinguish historical returns on the existing asset base from marginal returns expected on new projects; the second is crucial for evaluating future growth.

For practical analysis, show all three ratios over several periods, disclose definitions and explain major changes. Cross-check with cash conversion, maintenance expenditure and debt service. No company-specific ratio or current market ranking is established here. The synthetic calculations teach the framework and should be rebuilt from primary filings before supporting a real investment decision.

## Quick questions, clear answers

### Which ratio is best for every company?

None. ROE focuses on shareholders, while ROCE and ROIC examine operating capital under different conventions. Financial institutions and unusual capital structures may require adapted analysis. Choose the measure that matches the business question, then disclose the definition and limitations.

### Should I always exclude goodwill?

Not automatically. Including goodwill can assess returns on the full acquisition investment; excluding it can illuminate some operating comparisons. Both perspectives can be useful if clearly labelled. Removing goodwill without explanation may hide the cost of management's acquisition decisions.

### Is a rising ratio always good news?

No. It may reflect genuine improvement, an impairment, asset sales, reduced equity or temporary earnings. Reconcile numerator and denominator changes separately. A higher percentage deserves investigation into cash flow, reinvestment and financial risk before it is treated as evidence of better economics.
