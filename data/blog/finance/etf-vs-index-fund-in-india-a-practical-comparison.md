An ETF trades on an exchange during the market session, while a conventional index mutual fund is subscribed to or redeemed through the fund process at the applicable NAV. Both can track an index. Choose between them by comparing transaction convenience, execution costs, liquidity and tracking quality, rather than assuming the lower quoted expense ratio always wins.

## Separate the exposure from the wrapper

“Index” describes the investment approach; “ETF” describes an exchange-traded structure. When comparing Indian index products, first ensure both follow the same index and return version. Comparing a broad equity ETF with a sector index fund confuses the investment exposure with the way units are bought.

[SEBI's ETF guide](https://investor.sebi.gov.in/exchange_traded_fund.html) explains exchange trading and possible brokerage and demat costs. Retail purchases normally use exchange units rather than fractional units. A conventional index fund can accommodate rupee-based subscriptions, subject to its minimum amounts and operational rules.

For an ETF, you need the relevant trading and demat arrangements. For an index mutual fund, verify the authorised transaction channel, KYC and payment requirements. Your broker or platform may offer recurring facilities, but implementation and supported products vary; do not assume every ETF offers an identical SIP experience.

## Understand price, NAV and the spread

An ETF's exchange price is determined by available buy and sell orders. NAV represents the per-unit value of the underlying portfolio. These values can differ. The bid is what a buyer currently offers; the ask is what a seller currently requests. Their difference is the bid–ask spread.

Imagine a hypothetical bid of ₹99.80 and ask of ₹100.20. A buyer executing at the ask pays ₹100.20, while an immediate sale at an unchanged bid would receive ₹99.80 before charges. The ₹0.40 gap is an execution cost, even if the ETF's annual expense ratio is small.

A limit order controls the maximum purchase price or minimum sale price but does not guarantee execution. Examine available quantity near your intended price, not just the last traded price. A small trade long ago does not establish that a large current order can be executed cheaply.

## Calculate the costs you actually incur

Use a hypothetical ₹50,000 purchase. Suppose the ETF saves 0.2 percentage points annually in fund expenses compared with the index fund: the first-year saving on a constant balance would be about ₹100. If additional trading and account costs exceed that amount, the ETF may initially cost more overall.

This is a comparison framework, not a current quote. Obtain the actual brokerage, statutory transaction charges, demat fees, spreads and fund expenses applicable to your account and product. For the index fund, check applicable exit loads and the transaction rules. Compare costs over the expected holding period rather than just one purchase.

[The SEC's ETF expense bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/mutual-fund-and-etf-fees-and-expenses-investor-bulletin) distinguishes fund expenses from exchange execution costs and premiums or discounts. Its structural explanation is useful; its US-specific charges and tax treatment are not an Indian fee schedule.

## Match the structure to your investing habits

A conventional index fund can be convenient for recurring rupee investments without choosing an intraday execution price. You still need to understand applicable NAV, payment realisation and redemption settlement. Check current scheme documents instead of assuming all fund categories have identical rules.

An ETF can suit an investor who already uses a demat account and understands spreads and order execution. For substantial orders or thinly traded products, seek clarity on execution before trading. Secondary-market turnover alone does not describe the entire creation–redemption mechanism, but the price actually available to your order remains important.

Finally compare tracking difference over consistent periods. Securities lending, cash balances, rebalancing and expenses can affect realised index replication. Choose the wrapper you can operate accurately and affordably. Neither structure eliminates equity-market losses, and index selection matters more than small operational conveniences when the underlying exposures are fundamentally different.

## Quick questions, clear answers

### Can an ETF trade above its NAV?

Yes. Market price can show a premium or discount to portfolio value. Check current indicative information where available and actual market depth. Creation and redemption mechanisms can help alignment, but they do not guarantee that your particular order executes at NAV.

### Do I need a demat account for an index fund?

A conventional mutual fund holding need not rely on the same exchange-trading process as an ETF. Available holding modes and platform arrangements vary. Confirm how units will be recorded, how redemptions work and whether the chosen platform introduces additional account charges.

### Which is better for small monthly investments?

A conventional index fund may be simpler for fixed rupee contributions, while an ETF may work if execution and account costs are low. Calculate actual charges relative to the instalment size and confirm minimum units or subscription amounts before deciding.
