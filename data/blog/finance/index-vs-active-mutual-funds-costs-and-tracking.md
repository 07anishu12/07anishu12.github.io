Index funds aim to follow a specified index; active funds give managers discretion to select investments within a mandate. Compare the exposure you need, total costs and performance against an appropriate benchmark. There is no reliable promise that either approach will outperform in every category or period, and a lower fee alone does not describe every risk.

## Start with the investment mandate

An index is a rule-based basket, not an automatic guarantee of broad diversification. A sector index may be concentrated in one industry, while a broad market index spreads exposure more widely. Read the index methodology: selection rules, weighting, rebalancing and constituent limits influence what you actually own.

An active manager can overweight, underweight or avoid securities within the scheme's mandate. That discretion creates the possibility of outperforming the benchmark and the risk of underperforming it. The fund's stated process should explain how it selects investments, controls concentration and responds when an investment thesis fails.

[AMFI's scheme classification material](https://www.amfiindia.com/investor/knowledge-center-info?zoneName=CategorizationOfMutualFundSchemes) describes index funds as seeking index-linked exposure. In practice, costs and implementation mean realised fund returns can differ from the index. Avoid reading “tracks the index” as a promise of identical returns.

## Distinguish two tracking measures

Tracking difference is the return gap between a fund and its benchmark over a specified period. If the benchmark returns a hypothetical 12% and the fund returns 11.5%, defining the gap as fund minus benchmark gives a tracking difference of minus 0.5 percentage points. State the sign convention because reports may reverse it.

Tracking error measures the variability of periodic fund-minus-benchmark returns. It is commonly calculated as their standard deviation, annualised consistently with the observation frequency. A fund can trail by a fairly stable amount and have a negative tracking difference but low tracking error.

A low tracking error therefore does not prove that the fund is cheap. Compare the return shortfall too. Use the same benchmark, observation window, frequency and annualisation convention when comparing published figures. Otherwise apparent differences may reflect measurement choices rather than better implementation.

## Show the hurdle imposed by costs

Suppose two hypothetical funds earn 10% before expenses. If one loses 0.3 percentage points to annual costs and another loses 1.3, simplified net returns are 9.7% and 8.7%. On ₹1,00,000 over ten years, use `=100000*(1+rate)^10` to compare the outcome.

This illustration isolates costs; it does not assume active and index portfolios actually earn identical gross returns. An active strategy must generate sufficient additional gross performance to overcome its additional costs before producing a better net result for the investor.

The [SEC's investor fee explanation](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/mutual-fund-fees-and-expenses) supports the general principle that costs reduce investor returns. Indian plan expenses must be checked in current scheme disclosures; US fee categories and tax rules should not be transplanted into an Indian comparison.

## Evaluate performance without cherry-picking

Match an active fund with its stated benchmark and relevant category. A small-company fund should not be declared superior merely because it beat a large-company index in a favourable small-company market. Check whether returns include distributions and whether you are comparing direct or regular plans.

Use multiple rolling periods where reliable data is available. Examine drawdowns, portfolio concentration, turnover and changes in personnel or mandate. A single strong year can dominate a short track record. Excluding closed or merged funds can also make a historical comparison look better than the full investor experience.

Choose an index fund when you value a transparent rules-based exposure and accept the index's concentration. Consider an active fund only where you understand the process, costs and reasons it might succeed or fail. Build the asset allocation before multiplying fund names: several funds holding similar securities may add complexity without meaningful diversification.

## Quick questions, clear answers

### Can an index fund lose money?

Yes. It follows the underlying market exposure, including declines. Tracking a benchmark well does not protect principal. A concentrated index may also carry substantial sector or stock risk, so examine the holdings and methodology rather than relying on the passive label.

### Is the lowest expense ratio always best?

Compare tracking difference, liquidity arrangements, operational features and the exact index as well. Two funds with different indices are different exposures. Even within one index, a slightly lower quoted expense ratio does not guarantee the smallest realised tracking shortfall.

### What proves that active management is worthwhile?

There is no single decisive statistic. Consistent net performance, a credible repeatable process and risks aligned with your needs provide useful evidence, but cannot guarantee future results. Examine whether success depended on a temporary style tailwind or an unusually concentrated position.
