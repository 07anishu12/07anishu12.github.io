An existing fixed-rate bond becomes less attractive when comparable new bonds offer a higher required yield, so its market price normally falls. The price adjusts until its promised cash flows offer the market's required return. This inverse relationship assumes the relevant cash flows remain unchanged; credit quality, options and liquidity can move prices independently too.

## Distinguish coupon, price and yield

The coupon is the contractual interest amount or rate specified by the bond terms. A fixed coupon generally does not change simply because market rates move. Price is what a buyer pays in the secondary market. Yield relates that price to the future cash flows under a stated calculation.

If a bond promises ₹80 annual interest on ₹1,000 face value, its coupon rate is 8%. If it trades below ₹1,000, the same ₹80 is a larger percentage of the purchase price. Yield to maturity also accounts for principal repayment and the time remaining, not just coupon divided by price.

[The SEC's bond bulletin](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/what-are) explains interest-rate risk and the role of maturity and coupons. This guide uses the general mechanism, not US bond-market rules as an Indian trading specification.

## Calculate the adjustment with one payment

Consider a hypothetical one-year bond promising ₹1,080 at maturity, comprising ₹1,000 principal and ₹80 interest. If the required one-year yield is 8%, price is `1080/1.08`, or ₹1,000.

If the required yield rises to 10% with the promised payment unchanged, price becomes `1080/1.10`, approximately ₹981.82. A new buyer paying that amount earns 10% if the payment arrives as promised. The existing owner experiences a mark-to-market price decline, even though the coupon has not changed.

If the required yield instead falls to 6%, price becomes `1080/1.06`, approximately ₹1,018.87. A higher price reduces the return available from the fixed future payment. The calculation makes the inverse relationship visible without relying on a verbal rule alone.

These figures are synthetic and omit accrued interest, taxes, charges and default risk. Real bonds can use different payment frequencies and day-count conventions. Match those terms when calculating an actual clean or dirty price.

## Extend the logic across several payments

For a plain annual-coupon bond, `Price = Sum of Coupon_t/(1+y)^t + Face value/(1+y)^n`, where y is the consistently applied annual yield and n the remaining years. More distant payments are more affected by a change in the discount rate.

That is why longer maturity and lower coupons often increase interest-rate sensitivity when other features are comparable. Duration provides a more informative summary than maturity alone because it considers cash-flow timing and yield. It remains an approximation for price changes, especially for larger moves or bonds with embedded options.

Do not treat a policy-rate change as an identical shift in every bond yield. Market expectations, maturity, inflation risk, credit spreads and liquidity all affect the required return. A bond price may already reflect an anticipated policy decision before it is announced.

## Understand holding to maturity and fund exposure

For an individual plain bond held to maturity, the contractual payments remain due according to the terms, subject to issuer performance and any options. Selling before maturity exposes the investor to the then-current price. A guaranteed-looking face value is not protection against default or every contractual event.

Holding does not eliminate opportunity cost, inflation risk or reinvestment risk. Coupons received may be reinvested at different rates. Yield to maturity is not an unconditional realised return promise, particularly if coupons cannot be reinvested as assumed or the issuer fails to pay.

A bond fund's NAV reflects its portfolio values and trading. It does not ordinarily become a guaranteed face-value repayment to an investor simply because some underlying bonds mature. Examine the scheme's duration, holdings, credit quality and investment horizon rather than treating all debt funds like a single deposit.

Before buying, identify the cash flows, maturity, issuer risk, options, price basis and costs. Use a scenario for a yield rise and another for a decline. No current yield or interest-rate forecast is established here; the purpose is to understand the mechanism before interpreting market movements.

## Quick questions, clear answers

### Does a rate rise change my fixed coupon?

Normally the contractual fixed coupon stays the same. The secondary-market price changes as investors compare that coupon and principal repayment with prevailing yields. Floating-rate or structured instruments have different terms, so check the specific bond rather than applying the fixed-coupon example universally.

### Can bond prices fall when policy rates are unchanged?

Yes. Credit concerns, liquidity, inflation expectations or changes in longer-term market yields can reduce prices. The policy rate is only one influence. Analyse the issuer and relevant yield curve rather than attributing every price movement to the latest central-bank decision.

### Are government bonds free from price risk?

No. Even where default risk is low, market yields can change and affect prices before maturity. Inflation and reinvestment also matter. The type of risk being discussed must be named; low credit risk does not mean that the quoted market value cannot fall.
